\begin{abstract}
We study the branching heuristic of a Conflict-Driven Clause Learning solver
for the Boolean satisfiability problem when it is learned by reinforcement, as
in Graph-Q-SAT, where a graph network reads the bipartite graph of the formula
and is trained by Deep Q-Learning to reduce the number of decisions of the
solver. We add to the core block of that network two edge-featured graph
attention layers, which weight the neighbors of each node by learned
coefficients before the global attribute is updated, and we compare the
resulting model (GAT-Q-SAT) with Graph-Q-SAT at matched training set and
configuration, with two controls that separate the effect of the
attention from that of the added parameters. On graph coloring GAT-Q-SAT takes
fewer decisions than Graph-Q-SAT on all the families we test but the
smallest, where the two are level, and the difference tends to grow with the
size of the instances, since Graph-Q-SAT approaches the reference solver on the
largest ones. A wider Graph-Q-SAT does not close the gap,
attention in place of the sum of the messages is worse than Graph-Q-SAT
throughout, and attention in the pooling of the global attribute does not
improve GAT-Q-SAT. Trained on uniform random 3-SAT
and tested on graph coloring, GAT-Q-SAT is again the better of the two, and it appears to be the better one on some structured families not seen in
training. Yet, on satisfiable uniform random 3-SAT
Graph-Q-SAT is clearly superior, and on instances of this size neither model is
competitive with the solver in running time, each decision costing a forward
pass of the network. This evidence rests on five training seeds per model on
graph coloring (two or three per control), and one run per training family elsewhere. Computational results are reported.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%% ===========================================================================

We are concerned with the branching heuristic of a solver for the Boolean
satisfiability problem (SAT), i.e., the problem of deciding whether a formula in
conjunctive normal form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cnf}
  \varphi \;=\; \bigwedge_{j=1}^{m} \; \bigvee_{\ell \in C_j} \ell
\end{equation}
over $n$ Boolean variables admits an assignment that satisfies all the clauses
$C_j$ (each literal $\ell$ being a variable or its negation). We consider
solvers that implement Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL): the search
repeatedly (i) decides the value of an unassigned variable, (ii) propagates the
assignments implied by the clauses, and (iii) upon a conflict learns a new
clause and backjumps to the level that the clause allows
\cite{MarquesSilva1999,MarquesSilva2021}. In practice the choice at point (i)
is delegated to a heuristic, usually some variant of the Variable State Independent Decaying
Sum (VSIDS) \cite{Moskewicz2001} (see, e.g., \cite{Liang2016} for an
alternative), which ranks the variables by activity counters that are increased
at each conflict and periodically decayed. These counters usually carry little
information at the start of the search, and in any case they are only a rough
surrogate of what one would like to know (say, the variable whose assignment
leads to the smallest search tree). The heuristic is therefore a natural place for a learned
policy, and a convenient one, since a poor decision costs time but not
correctness (the solver is sound and complete whatever the policy does). We
restrict ourselves to the choice of the variable and of its value, and we leave
restarts, clause deletion and preprocessing as they are in MiniSat
\cite{EenSorensson2003}.

\smallskip
\noindent
Learning for SAT has a large literature, which cannot be reviewed here; we
refer to \cite{Guo2023} for a survey and to \cite{Li2024} for a benchmark of
graph networks on SAT problems, and only recall the works that bear on ours.
NeuroSAT \cite{Selsam2019} predicts satisfiability from single-bit
supervision, which shows that message passing \cite{Gilmer2017} over the graph
of a formula captures some of its structure. NeuroCore \cite{SelsamBjorner2019}
predicts the variables that occur in unsatisfiable cores and uses the
prediction to re-rank the VSIDS activities periodically, while NeuroBack
\cite{Wang2024} queries the network once, before the search, so that inference
is paid offline. Other works learn heuristics for local search
\cite{YolcuPoczos2019,Zhang2020}. Learning the branching rule by reinforcement
goes back at least to \cite{LagoudakisLittman2001}, and the
analogous problem for Mixed-Integer Programs has been approached with
imitation learning and graph networks \cite{Khalil2016,Gasse2019} (cf.
\cite{Bengio2021} for a survey of learning in combinatorial optimization).
Graph-Q-SAT \cite{Kurin2020}, on which we build, represents the state of the
search as the bipartite graph of the formula, and learns the action-value
function of the corresponding decision process with Deep Q-Learning (DQN)
\cite{Mnih2015}. Since the network reads a graph, one set of weights applies to
formulas of any size, and the heuristic can be evaluated on instances larger
than (or different from) the training ones. This model has been extended with
new graph representations and with ``restricted'' heuristics that query the
network on part of the decisions only \cite{Shirokikh2023}, and
\cite{Mojzisek2026} studies how much of the reported gains depends on the
experimental choices around the method. Graph attention
\cite{Velickovic2018,Brody2022} appears in some of the works above but, to the
best of our knowledge, not inside the core block of Graph-Q-SAT.

\smallskip
\noindent
In this note we add to the core block of Graph-Q-SAT two graph attention layers
that use the edge embeddings computed by the block, and we compare the
resulting model (GAT-Q-SAT) with Graph-Q-SAT. We make the comparison at
matched training set and configuration, on graph coloring (from instances of
the size of the training ones to instances four times larger), on uniform
random 3-SAT and, with no retraining, on thirteen structured SATLIB families
that neither model has seen, together with small-world coloring at nine levels
of structure. Since the attention layers are added to the block (the sum
aggregation of Graph-Q-SAT being kept), GAT-Q-SAT has about 60\% more
parameters than Graph-Q-SAT. In order to separate the effect of the
attention from that of the capacity we also train two controls, i.e.,
Graph-Q-SAT with wider hidden layers and the same number of parameters as
GAT-Q-SAT (Graph-Q-SAT wide), and a model in which attention replaces the sum
at the capacity of Graph-Q-SAT (attention aggregation), and we try a variant
of GAT-Q-SAT in which attention also replaces the mean that pools the nodes and
the edges into the global attribute (GAT-Q-SAT pool). We use five training
seeds for each of the two main models on graph coloring, three for GAT-Q-SAT
pool and two for each control, which is enough to see the larger
differences but not always the smaller ones (i.e., those of a few tenths of the
measure used below). As in \cite{Kurin2020} the results are measured
in solver iterations, and the running time is reported separately. The note
also documents a port of the original implementation to a current software
stack (without which the experiments could not have been run), together with
the test used to check that the port has not changed what the models compute.
The code of both models and of the experiments is available at
\url{https://github.com/dmeoli/GATQSAT}.

\smallskip
\noindent
The structure of the note is as follows. In section~\ref{sec:problem} we recall
the decision process defined by the branching problem and the algorithm used to
learn it, with the training configuration of all the runs. Section~\ref{sec:gqsat}
describes the graph representation of the state and the network of Graph-Q-SAT,
and section~\ref{sec:gat} the attention layers that GAT-Q-SAT adds to it; the
port and the reproduction test are described in section~\ref{sec:impl}. In
section~\ref{sec:results} we report the computational results, ordered by the
distance between the training and the test instances (graph coloring at
increasing size, with the two controls, a change of the training distribution,
cross-domain transfer), followed by uniform random 3-SAT and by the running time. Finally, in
section~\ref{sec:conc} we draw some conclusions, and the appendix describes the
instances and how the numbers of section~\ref{sec:results} are regenerated.
Instance families keep their SATLIB \cite{HoosStutzle2000} names:
\code{uf}$n$-$m$ and \code{uuf}$n$-$m$ are satisfiable and unsatisfiable
uniform random 3-SAT with $n$ variables and $m$ clauses, and \code{flat}$n$-$m$
are the 3-coloring problems of graphs with $n$ nodes and $m$ edges, generated
as in \cite{CulbersonLuo1996}.

\section{Branching as a decision process}\label{sec:problem}
%% ===========================================================================

Following \cite{Kurin2020}, the solver is the environment of a Markov Decision
Process. Its state is the formula \eqref{eq:cnf} simplified by the current
partial assignment (together with the clauses learned so far), and its actions
are the pairs of an unassigned variable and a truth value. Transitions are
computed by the solver itself (cf. \cite[section 3]{Kurin2020}): a step is one decision followed by the propagation, the
conflict analysis and the backjumping that it triggers, and therefore the
transition is deterministic but known only by running it (the formula is
first simplified by the variable elimination of MiniSat, which is part of the
environment). A reward of $-0.1$
is given at each non-terminal step and $0$ at termination, which makes the
return a function of the length of the episode alone.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:return}
Let $\gamma \in (0, 1)$, and let an episode terminate after $T \ge 1$ steps.
Then its discounted return is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:return}
  G_T \;=\; -0.1 \sum_{t=0}^{T-2} \gamma^t \;=\; -0.1\, \frac{1 - \gamma^{T-1}}{1 - \gamma} \, ,
\end{equation}
which is strictly decreasing in $T$. Hence, on a given formula, of two
deterministic policies the one that terminates in fewer steps has the larger
return.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The first $T-1$ rewards are $-0.1$ and the last one is $0$, which gives
\eqref{eq:return}; each further step adds the negative term $-0.1\,\gamma^{T-1}$.
Since the transitions are deterministic, so is the episode of a deterministic
policy on a given formula.
\end{proof}

\noindent
Note that \eqref{eq:return} is bounded below by $-0.1/(1 - \gamma)$, and hence
for $\gamma = 0.99$ (cf. below) two episodes of a few hundred steps have
almost the same return ($\gamma^{300} < 0.05$): the reward separates a ``short''
episode from a ``long'' one much better than two long ones. Also, the lemma counts
the steps of the model only, and the decisions that VSIDS takes after the cap
(see below) do not enter the return.

\smallskip
\noindent
The action-value function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bellman}
  Q^\star(s,a) = \mathbb{E}\big[\,r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^\star(s',a')\,\big]
\end{equation}
is approximated by a network $Q_\theta$ trained by DQN \cite{Mnih2015}, which
minimizes the squared one-step temporal-difference error on minibatches drawn
uniformly from a replay buffer, with the bootstrap target computed by a copy
$\theta^-$ of the weights that is refreshed periodically, and with the
transitions collected by an $\varepsilon$-greedy policy (neither the double
estimator of \cite{vanHasselt2016} nor the prioritized replay of
\cite{Schaul2016} is used). The learned heuristic is then
$\pi(s) = \arg\max_a Q_\theta(s,a)$. Two features of the problem constrain the
approximator: the number of actions changes along an episode (two per
unassigned variable), and the state is defined up to a permutation of the
variables and of the clauses. Hence, the network must score a variable
number of actions and be invariant under those permutations, which rules out
fixed-size encodings such as that of \cite{WangRompf2018} and leads to the
graph representation of section~\ref{sec:gqsat}.

\smallskip
\noindent
All the runs of this note use the configuration of \cite{Kurin2020}, which we
list here since it is not repeated elsewhere. Training uses Adam with a
constant learning rate of $2\cdot 10^{-5}$, the discount is $\gamma = 0.99$,
the replay buffer holds 20000 transitions and the minibatches have 64 of them.
One update is made every 4 transitions after the first 5000, and $\theta$ is copied
into $\theta^-$ every 10 updates. Training stops after 50000 updates (about
$2\cdot 10^{5}$ transitions). The exploration rate $\varepsilon$ is $1$ for the
first 5000 transitions, then decreases linearly (by $0.99/30000$ per
transition) until transition 30000, where it is $0.175$. From there on it is
$0.01$; this jump comes from the schedule of the released code, which compares
the length of the decay with the total number of transitions. During training
an episode may take at most 500 decisions of the model (the \emph{cap}), after
which the solver completes the search with VSIDS in a single step; at test
time the cap is a parameter of the evaluation (cf. section~\ref{sec:results}).
Also, the gradient norm is clipped at $0.1$
for the runs trained on graph coloring and at $1$ for those trained on uniform
random 3-SAT (as in the respective configurations of \cite{Kurin2020}).

\section{Graph-Q-SAT}\label{sec:gqsat}
%% ===========================================================================

The state is represented \cite{Kurin2020} as a bipartite graph whose nodes are
the unassigned variables and the clauses (original and learned) that are not
yet satisfied, each clause restricted to its literals that are not false. There
is an edge between a variable and each clause in which it occurs (stored in
both directions). Node features are 2-dimensional one-hot vectors that
distinguish the two kinds of node, edge features encode in the same way the
polarity of the literal, and the global attribute is a constant. The network
produces two $Q$-values on each variable node (one per truth value), and the
agent takes the $\arg\max$ over all of them.

\smallskip
\noindent
As in \cite{Kurin2020}, the network is an encode--process--decode graph network
\cite{Battaglia2018} (cf. Figure~\ref{fig:epd}). An encoder maps the edges, the
nodes and the global attribute independently, each by a linear layer followed
by a ReLU and a layer normalization, to embeddings of size 32. Then, the core
block (whose weights are shared) is applied $M = 4$ times to the concatenation
of the encoded graph and of its previous output (zero at the first round). We
denote by $e_{ij}$ the embedding of the edge from node $i$ to node $j$ (one for
each direction), by $v_i$ that of node $i$, by $u$ the
global attribute, and by $\mathcal N_i$ the set of the neighbors of $i$ in the
bipartite graph. Each application of the block updates first the edges, then
the nodes, then the global attribute:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gnblock}
  e_{ij}' = \phi^e\big(e_{ij}, v_i, v_j, u\big) \, , \quad
  v_i' = \phi^v\Big(\textstyle\sum_{j \in \mathcal N_i} e_{ij}', \, v_i, \, u\Big) \, , \quad
  u'   = \phi^u\big(\bar e', \bar v', u\big) \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\bar e'$ and $\bar v'$ are the means of the updated edge and node
embeddings, and $\phi^e$, $\phi^v$ and $\phi^u$ are perceptrons with one hidden
layer of 64 units and outputs of size 64, each followed by a ReLU and a layer
normalization. Finally, a decoder of the same form as the encoder maps the
embedding of each variable node to 32 components, and a linear layer maps
these to the two $Q$-values of the node. Since the weights are shared across
the nodes and across the $M$ rounds, one set of them applies to a formula with
any number of variables and clauses. With this configuration the network has
71144 parameters.

\smallskip
\noindent
The network has the invariance required in section~\ref{sec:problem}, which
we state formally since the variants of section~\ref{sec:gat} must preserve it.
Let $\sigma$ be a permutation of the variables and $\tau$ one of the clauses
of a formula. We denote by $G^{\sigma\tau}$ the graph obtained from the graph
$G$ of the state by renaming its nodes accordingly (the features of the nodes
and of the edges being carried along), and by $Q_\theta(G, x, b)$ the
$Q$-value that the network computes on $G$ for the variable $x$ and the truth
value $b$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:inv}
For all $\sigma$, $\tau$, $x$ and $b$ one has
$Q_\theta(G^{\sigma\tau}, \sigma(x), b) = Q_\theta(G, x, b)$; hence, up to the
rule that breaks the ties, $\pi$ takes the same decision on $G$ and on
$G^{\sigma\tau}$, after the renaming.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The encoder and the decoder act on each edge, node and global attribute
separately, and so do $\phi^e$, $\phi^v$ and $\phi^u$ given their arguments.
In \eqref{eq:gnblock} a node enters the arguments of $\phi^v$ only through the
sum over $\mathcal N_i$, and the global update only through the means over all
the edges and nodes, none of which depends on the order of its terms. Hence, by
induction on the $M$ applications of the block, the embedding of each element
of $G^{\sigma\tau}$ is that of the corresponding element of $G$.
\end{proof} (The figures follow the notation of \cite{Battaglia2018},
in which $V$ and $E$ are the sets of the node and edge embeddings and $\rho$
denotes an aggregation, e.g., the sum in \eqref{eq:gnblock}.)

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{gqsat_epd.png}
\caption{The encode--process--decode network of Graph-Q-SAT: an independent
block encodes the edges, the nodes and the global attribute, a full graph-network
block is applied $M$ times with shared weights, and a second independent block
decodes the $Q$-values. Blocks drawn after \cite{Battaglia2018}.}
\label{fig:epd}
\end{figure}

\smallskip
\noindent
Note that the node update of \eqref{eq:gnblock} sums the messages of the
edges incident to a node, and therefore it gives the same weight to all the clauses in which a
variable occurs (and to all the variables of a clause). For a formula drawn
uniformly at random at the phase transition \cite{Mitchell1992} this seems a
reasonable choice, the clauses being statistically alike. In a formula that
encodes a structured problem, instead, the clauses of a variable often play
different roles (in graph coloring, e.g., ``at least one color'' for a node
and ``different colors'' for the two ends of an edge), and one may expect a
weighting of the neighbors to be of some use.

\section{GAT-Q-SAT}\label{sec:gat}
%% ===========================================================================

GAT-Q-SAT inserts two graph attention layers \cite{Velickovic2018} in the core
block, between the node update and the global update of \eqref{eq:gnblock}.
Thus, the node embeddings are recomputed from those of their neighbors, with
learned weights, before $u'$ is computed. Each layer maps the embeddings $v_i$
of the nodes (the first one those computed by $\phi^v$) into
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gat}
  \begin{gathered}
  v_i^{+} = \mathop{\Big\|}_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{j \in \mathcal N_i} \alpha^k_{ij}\, W_k v_j + b \, ,
  \qquad
  \alpha^k_{ij} = \frac{\exp \omega^k_{ij}}{\sum_{j' \in \mathcal N_i} \exp \omega^k_{ij'}} \, ,
  \\
  \omega^k_{ij} = \mathrm{LeakyReLU}\big(\mathbf w_k^{\!\top}
            [\,W_k v_i \,\|\, W_k v_j \,\|\, W^e_k e'_{ji}\,]\big) \, ,
  \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where $K$ is the number of heads, $\|$ denotes concatenation, the LeakyReLU has
slope $0.2$ on the negative values, and $e'_{ji}$ is the embedding of the edge
from $j$ to $i$ computed by $\phi^e$ in the same application of the block (no
self-loop is added, since the graph is bipartite). Hence, the coefficients
$\alpha^k_{ij}$ depend on the current edge embeddings, which carry the polarity
of the literal together with what the block has added to it. In our
configuration the first layer has $K = 3$ heads of size 64 (concatenated into
192 components) and the second a single head, which returns to size 64; each
layer is followed by a ReLU, and the output by a layer normalization. (In the
notation of Figure~\ref{fig:gatepd}, $\alpha^e$ are the coefficients
$\alpha^k_{ij}$ and $\beta^e$ the messages $W_k v_j$.) All the rest of the
network (the encoder, the edge and node updates
with their sum, the global update, the decoder, and $M$) is unchanged, and so is
the training configuration of section~\ref{sec:problem}. In the terms of
\cite{Battaglia2018}, the resulting block is a full graph-network block with a
non-local block inserted between the node and the global update
(cf. Figure~\ref{fig:gatepd}).

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{gatqsat_epd.png}
\caption{The core block of GAT-Q-SAT: the block of Figure~\ref{fig:epd} with a
non-local block of two attention layers \eqref{eq:gat} inserted between the
node and the global update (in violet the edge and node blocks and the two
arrows that bring their outputs to the attention layers, in ochre the global
block and the arrow that brings the output of the attention layers before
it). Blocks drawn after \cite{Battaglia2018}.}
\label{fig:gatepd}
\end{figure}

\smallskip
\noindent
The attention layers add 42112 parameters to the 71144 of Graph-Q-SAT (about
60\% more), and their cost is paid at each decision, since the coefficients are
computed on all the edges at each application of the block. Of course, the
coefficients can only be of use where the neighbors of a node differ in a way
that matters for the decision. On a uniform random formula at the phase
transition one may expect them to learn little (if anything), and the added
parameters to make the fit somewhat harder.

\smallskip
\noindent
{\bf Controls.} Since the layers are added and the sum of \eqref{eq:gnblock} is
kept, a difference between GAT-Q-SAT and Graph-Q-SAT may be due to the attention
or simply to the larger network. We therefore train two further models with the
same configuration. The first one (Graph-Q-SAT wide) is Graph-Q-SAT with hidden
layers of 104 units in place of 64, which gives 113504 parameters (against the
113256 of GAT-Q-SAT) and no attention. The second one (attention aggregation)
keeps the block of \eqref{eq:gnblock} and replaces its sum by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:attnagg}
  \begin{gathered}
  \sum_{j \in \mathcal N_i} \bar\lambda_{ij}\, e_{ij}' \, , \qquad
  \bar\lambda_{ij} = \frac1K \sum_{k=1}^{K} \lambda^k_{ij} \, ,
  \\
  \lambda^k_{ij} = \frac{\exp \mu^k_{ij}}{\sum_{j' \in \mathcal N_i} \exp \mu^k_{ij'}} \, , \qquad
  \mu^k_{ij} = \mathrm{LeakyReLU}\big(\mathbf z_k^{\!\top} [\, v_i \,\|\, e_{ij}' \,]\big) \, ,
  \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
with $K = 3$ heads, whose coefficients are averaged so that $\phi^v$ receives an
input of the same size as with the sum. The vectors $\mathbf z_k$ (each with a
bias, which the normalization cancels) add 483 parameters to the 71144 of
Graph-Q-SAT. The first control has the capacity of GAT-Q-SAT without its
attention, and the second one has the attention (in a simpler form) without
the added capacity. However, a weighted mean such as \eqref{eq:attnagg} does not carry
the degree of the node, which the sum does, as the following lemma shows
(cf. \cite{Xu2019}, where the same fact is shown for the plain mean).

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:degree}
Let $i$ and $i'$ be two nodes with $v_i = v_{i'}$, and let the multiset of the
edge embeddings $e'_{i'j}$, $j \in \mathcal N_{i'}$, be that of the
$e'_{ij}$, $j \in \mathcal N_i$, with each element repeated $k \ge 1$ times
(so that $|\mathcal N_{i'}| = k\, |\mathcal N_i|$). Then \eqref{eq:attnagg}
gives the same vector at $i$ and at $i'$, while the sum of \eqref{eq:gnblock}
gives at $i'$ $k$ times its value at $i$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The score $\mu^k_{ij}$ depends on $j$ only through $e'_{ij}$, and hence the
$k$ copies of a term at $i'$ have the score of that term at $i$; the
denominator of $\lambda^k_{i'j}$ is $k$ times that of $\lambda^k_{ij}$, so that
each copy has $1/k$ of the coefficient of the original term, and the $k$
copies add up to it. The sum, instead, is linear in the multiplicity of its
terms.
\end{proof}

\noindent
At the first application of the block, for instance, all the edges of a
variable with the same polarity have the same embedding, and \eqref{eq:attnagg}
then cannot tell a variable that occurs in two clauses from one that occurs in
twenty with the same proportion of polarities. This may matter on graphs whose
degrees vary, and it is one of the reasons why the second control is not simply
a smaller GAT-Q-SAT, in which the attention layers act on embeddings already
computed by the sum.

\smallskip
\noindent
In both Graph-Q-SAT and GAT-Q-SAT the global update of \eqref{eq:gnblock}
receives the plain means $\bar e'$ and $\bar v'$, so that the attribute $u$, which
enters the update of all the edges and nodes at the next application of the block,
weighs all the variables and clauses of the formula alike. A third model
(GAT-Q-SAT pool) is GAT-Q-SAT with these two means replaced by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:attpool}
  \begin{gathered}
  \sum_{i} \bar\gamma_i\, v_i' \, , \qquad
  \bar\gamma_i = \frac1K \sum_{k=1}^{K} \gamma^k_i \, ,
  \\
  \gamma^k_i = \frac{\exp \eta^k_i}{\sum_{i'} \exp \eta^k_{i'}} \, , \qquad
  \eta^k_i = \mathrm{LeakyReLU}\big(\mathbf y_k^{\!\top} [\, v_i' \,\|\, u \,]\big) \, ,
  \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where $v_i'$ are the node embeddings that enter the global update (i.e., those
returned by the attention layers), the sums run over all the nodes of the
graph, and $K = 3$; the mean of the edges is replaced in the same way, with
vectors of its own. These add 966 parameters to the 113256 of GAT-Q-SAT. The
model lets the global attribute summarize the formula by the variables and
clauses that the network deems relevant, instead of all of them alike; unlike
the two controls, it is meant as a possible improvement of GAT-Q-SAT, not as a
test of it. All the three variants keep the invariance of
Proposition~\ref{prop:inv}. In fact, \eqref{eq:gat}, \eqref{eq:attnagg} and
\eqref{eq:attpool} replace a sum or a mean by a sum over the same set whose
coefficients are a softmax over that set, which is permuted together with its
arguments; hence, the induction of the proof goes through unchanged.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}
%% ===========================================================================

The original implementation of \cite{Kurin2020} is written in PyTorch~1.1 with pinned
versions of PyTorch Geometric \cite{FeyLenssen2019} and of the compiled
extensions \code{torch-scatter} and \code{torch-sparse}. It drives a modified
MiniSat~2.2 \cite{EenSorensson2003}, exposed to Python as a \code{gym}
environment through a SWIG wrapper. These versions can no longer be installed
together on a current system, and therefore we ported the code to current
releases of PyTorch and PyTorch Geometric. In the port the aggregations of
\eqref{eq:gnblock} are taken from \code{torch\_geometric.utils.scatter}, which
removes the two compiled extensions. The environment is constructed
directly, without \code{gym.make} (whose checks reject the non-standard
signatures of the MiniSat wrapper), which allows the same code to run with
\code{gym} and with \code{gymnasium}; the SWIG wrapper is regenerated and
rebuilt against \code{numpy}~2. Also, the version of PyTorch Geometric of the original implementation
stores the linear map of an attention layer twice (\code{lin\_src} and
\code{lin\_dst}), where the current one has a single \code{lin}. When a
checkpoint written by that version is loaded one of the two copies is kept; this loses nothing,
since in all our checkpoints the two are equal.

\smallskip
\noindent
In order to check that the port has not changed what the models compute, we
re-ran on the new stack the evaluation of the checkpoints trained on the original one, and compared the logs instance by instance. In 63 of the 86
repeated evaluations the number of iterations of the model coincides with the original one on all the instances; in the other 23 the median over the instances
of the ratio between the iterations of MiniSat and those of the model (the
measure \eqref{eq:mrir} of section~\ref{sec:results}) changes by at most $0.03$
(on \code{flat30-60} at cap $500$,
e.g., the medians are $1.833$ for Graph-Q-SAT and $2.000$ for GAT-Q-SAT on both
stacks). We have not traced the residual differences, which may be due to the
non-determinism of the scatter operations on a GPU; they are in any case much
smaller than the differences discussed in section~\ref{sec:results}. Indeed, inference
exercises the graph construction, the message passing, the decoder and the
stepping of the environment together, and hence this is a reasonably stringent
test of all but the training path. The latter was checked by retraining the two
coloring models from scratch on the new stack (four of the five seeds of each of them in section~\ref{sec:results} are
trained in this way).

\smallskip
\noindent
Finally, the evaluation code implements the three restricted heuristics of
\cite{Shirokikh2023}, which reduce the number of forward passes. In the first
one the model takes the first $T$ decisions and VSIDS the rest. In the second
one a forward pass gives the $L$ best actions, which are executed in order
(skipping those whose variable has meanwhile been assigned) before the network
is queried again. In the third one the $Q$-values computed at the root
initialize the activities of VSIDS, which then runs unaided (this required a
new method of the solver, which writes the activities and rebuilds the order
heap). We do not report results for them, since on the instances of
section~\ref{sec:results} the search is too short for the saving in inference
to show.

%% ===========================================================================
\section{Computational results}\label{sec:results}
%% ===========================================================================

We now compare the two models on test instances that are increasingly far from
the training ones. As in \cite{Kurin2020}, the quality of a heuristic is
measured by the Median Relative Iteration Reduction
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mrir}
  \MRIR = \operatorname{median}\,
          \frac{\#\text{iterations of MiniSat}}{\#\text{iterations of the model}} \, ,
\end{equation}
where the median is over the instances of a family, and an iteration is a
branching decision (of the model or of VSIDS). Each evaluation is run at a
given cap (cf. section~\ref{sec:problem}), after which VSIDS takes the
remaining decisions; unless stated otherwise the cap is $500$, as in training.

\smallskip
\noindent
{\bf Reference solver.} The model-guided solver runs without
restarts, while MiniSat can be run with or without them (with the restart
sequence of \cite{Luby1993}), and the choice of the denominator of
\eqref{eq:mrir} is by no means inconsequential. On graph coloring MiniSat with
restarts takes up to 26\% fewer iterations than without (on
\code{flat175-417}); on \code{uf250-1065} and \code{uuf250-1065}, however, it
takes 2.2 and 2.9 times more. We divide by the stronger of the two on each
family, i.e., by MiniSat with restarts on graph coloring (as in
\cite{Kurin2020}) and without restarts on uniform random 3-SAT. The one
exception is \code{uuf100-430}, where the run with restarts takes 6\% fewer
iterations but where the logs of the runs trained on random formulas record only the
other one; hence, the MRIR reported there is slightly
favorable to the models. On the families of
section~\ref{sec:transfer} we also divide by the stronger of the two.

\smallskip
\noindent
{\bf Runs.} Each run is trained once on one family, with the configuration of
section~\ref{sec:problem}, and is evaluated at its last checkpoint. On graph
coloring, Graph-Q-SAT and GAT-Q-SAT have five runs each trained on
\code{flat50-115} (i.e., five seeds), one of them trained with the original implementation and
four with the port of section~\ref{sec:impl}; the two controls of
section~\ref{sec:gat} have two runs each, and GAT-Q-SAT pool has three. On uniform random 3-SAT each variant has two runs trained on satisfiable
formulas (one on \code{uf50-218}, one on \code{uf100-430}), whose results we
average, and two trained on the unsatisfiable \code{uuf50-218} and
\code{uuf100-430}, used only for a comparison in section~\ref{sec:random}. For a
group of runs we report the mean of the MRIR of its runs.

\smallskip
\noindent
{\bf Instances and machine.} Each test set has 100 instances (101 for
\code{flat150-360}): the held-out test part for the families used in training,
and the whole family for the others (cf. the appendix). The evaluations were run on a cloud
instance with an NVIDIA T4 GPU, except those of the fifth seed of Graph-Q-SAT
and of GAT-Q-SAT and those of GAT-Q-SAT pool, which were run on the CPUs of a
server with two AMD EPYC 9654 processors (4 threads per evaluation). The MRIR,
a ratio of iteration counts, does not depend on the machine, while running
times are only compared on the same machine (cf. section~\ref{sec:time}).

is about $0.25$ above it on \code{flat125-301}. Hence, also with two seeds
there is no indication that the attention in the pooling helps (although two
seeds may well hide a difference of a few tenths).

\subsection{Cross-domain transfer}\label{sec:transfer}

All the families above are random, either in the formula or in the graph, and
therefore they are of limited help to a user of a learned heuristic, who would
like to know whether a heuristic trained on the instances at hand is of any use
on different ones. We therefore evaluated the coloring-trained models (all their
seeds, with no retraining) on the structured families of SATLIB. We took all of
them, with the exception of those whose instances exceed 2000 variables or
150000 clauses (the size of the largest instances that we could evaluate on our
hardware in reasonable time), and an instance enters the panel only if MiniSat,
with and without restarts, solves it within 10 minutes (cf. the appendix). The resulting
panel has thirteen families and 257 instances: AIM \cite{Asahiro1996},
\code{jnh}, \code{dubois}, \code{pret}, pigeon hole, parity, all interval
series, inductive inference, circuit fault analysis, the Beijing set, towers of
Hanoi, planning \cite{KautzSelman1996} and quasigroup completion
\cite{GomesSelman1997}. Several of these families are rather ``small'' (seven of
them have fewer than 10 instances), and on some of them MiniSat needs very few decisions after its
preprocessing (fewer than 10 on 46\% of the AIM instances, e.g.); we report the
MRIR on all the instances, as in \cite{Kurin2020}, and we also check that the
ranking does not change when those instances are left out. We evaluate at a cap
of $200$ to contain the cost of the largest instances, and divide by the
stronger MiniSat run on each family.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{transfer.png}
\caption{Coloring-trained models on thirteen SATLIB families not seen in
training (number of instances in parentheses): MRIR at cap $200$ against the
stronger MiniSat run on each family, mean over the seeds, on a log scale; bars
start at parity.}
\label{fig:transfer}
\end{figure}

\noindent
On \code{dubois} and \code{pret}, all of whose instances MiniSat solves in
fewer than 10 decisions, the MRIR of both models is $1$, and these two families
carry no information on the heuristic. On the other eleven GAT-Q-SAT is
above the reference solver on eight families (from $1.03$ on inductive
inference to $1.90$ on AIM) and Graph-Q-SAT on six (from $1.02$ to $1.23$ on
the Beijing set). GAT-Q-SAT is the better of the two on eight families, by
$0.04$ to $1.01$ (and by more than $0.1$ on seven of them), Graph-Q-SAT on
inductive inference (by $0.03$) and on towers of Hanoi (by $0.46$), and the two
are level on circuit fault analysis. Leaving out the instances that MiniSat
solves in fewer than 10 decisions changes this ranking only on circuit fault
analysis, where Graph-Q-SAT moves ahead ($1.05$ against $0.96$); on AIM,
instead, the gap widens ($3.12$ against $1.20$). We remark, however, that the
variation across the seeds is large: the values of the seeds of the two models
overlap on all the families, and on the smallest ones they often differ by more than the gap between the models (on all interval series, e.g., the seeds of
GAT-Q-SAT range from $0.20$ to $2.85$, and on the single instance of towers of
Hanoi those of Graph-Q-SAT from $0.37$ to $2.82$). Of the other three variants
(also shown in Figure~\ref{fig:transfer}), only Graph-Q-SAT wide is better than
GAT-Q-SAT on any of these families, i.e., on inductive inference ($1.75$
against $1.03$), and attention aggregation is below parity on eight of them. The panel therefore suggests that GAT-Q-SAT transfers
somewhat better to structured families, rather than showing it, and the
families with fewer than 10 instances are to be read as indications only.

\smallskip
\noindent
Small-world coloring \cite{Walsh1999} allows a more controlled experiment,
since it varies the structure of the graph within one problem. Its instances
are the 5-coloring problems of graphs on 100 vertices obtained from a ring
lattice by rewiring each edge with probability $p$, and SATLIB provides nine levels, from $p = 1$ (a
random graph) to $p = 2^{-8}$ (almost the lattice), plus the lattice itself.
Figure~\ref{fig:sw} reports the MRIR of the coloring-trained models on 20
instances per level (one for the lattice). The MRIR of both models
decreases with $p$. Graph-Q-SAT is above parity for $p \ge 2^{-2}$ (from $2.18$
to $1.24$), and GAT-Q-SAT only at $p = 1$ ($1.04$), being slightly below it at
$p = 2^{-1}$ and $2^{-2}$ ($0.97$ and $0.95$); both are below parity from
$p = 2^{-3}$ on, and about $0.04$ at $p = 2^{-8}$. Up to $p = 2^{-6}$ the
decisions of MiniSat do not decrease (their median is between 99 and 239 at each level), and hence the decline is due to the models, which take more and
more decisions as the graphs move away from the random ones of the training
family. At $p = 2^{-7}$, $2^{-8}$ and on the lattice, instead, MiniSat needs
only 11 to 17 decisions, and the MRIR falls also because the reference solver
becomes much faster. Graph-Q-SAT is ahead at the three highest levels, i.e., on
the graphs closest to those of training, by $0.29$ to $1.14$ (although one of
the seeds of GAT-Q-SAT reaches $2.29$ at $p = 1$); below them the two models
are level within $0.14$. On this family, then, the attention does not
help as the graphs become regular, and neither model is of use on graphs close
to the lattice.

However, the advantage of Graph-Q-SAT does not carry over to the
unsatisfiable formulas. On these the two models are level at 50 variables
($1.61$ and $1.62$), and GAT-Q-SAT is ahead at 100 ($1.69$ against $1.45$) and
at 250 ($1.45$ against $1.21$). Two explanations are possible, and our
measurements do not separate them: either Graph-Q-SAT has learned something
about where the satisfying assignments of these formulas tend to be, which is
of no use when there are none, or proving unsatisfiability leaves little room
to the branching heuristic, and the two models end up somewhat closer. The runs
trained on unsatisfiable formulas improve Graph-Q-SAT (from $1.61$ to $1.96$ on
\code{uuf50-218} and from $1.45$ to $1.56$ on \code{uuf100-430}) but not
GAT-Q-SAT ($1.63$ and $1.50$), which is consistent with the first explanation
without excluding the second. All in all, the regime in which the sum of
Graph-Q-SAT is clearly superior is that of satisfiable uniform random formulas,
which is also the one with the least structure for a weighting of the neighbors
to exploit.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Iterations and running time}\label{sec:time}

The MRIR counts iterations, which are the quantity on which the heuristic acts.
However, a decision of the model costs a forward pass of the network on the
current graph, and the attention layers make that pass more expensive. We
measure the time per instance around the episode. It includes the
parsing of the formula, the variable elimination, the construction of the graph
at each decision, the forward passes and the part of the search left to VSIDS
(but not the loading of the model and the runs of MiniSat that give
the denominator of \eqref{eq:mrir}, which are computed once per family and
stored with it). We report medians over the instances, since a few instances
usually take much longer than the others. The times are those measured on the GPU
instance, whose load was not under our control. Repeating two of the
evaluations (one seed per model on \code{flat200-479} at cap $500$) gave the same iterations on every instance
and medians within 5\% of the first ones ($4.6$\% and $2.4$\%), and hence we
only comment on differences much larger than that.

Table~\ref{tab:budget} reports time and MRIR on the largest coloring family at
three values of the cap. At cap $50$ a decision of GAT-Q-SAT is more expensive
than one of Graph-Q-SAT ($3.74$ against $2.44$ seconds for the same number
of model decisions), as expected. At larger caps, instead, GAT-Q-SAT is the
faster of the two (by a factor of $1.4$ at cap $500$ and of $2$ at cap $1000$), since it terminates in fewer decisions; indeed, each decision of a
model costs far more than the VSIDS decisions it replaces. Graph-Q-SAT pays
$12$ times the time of cap $50$ to reach a lower MRIR at cap $1000$ ($1.17$
against $1.61$), and one of its seeds ends up below the reference solver
there. Going from cap $50$ to cap $500$ improves
GAT-Q-SAT by $0.19$, and going to $1000$ changes nothing, since its episodes
terminate before the cap is reached.

\smallskip
\noindent
In absolute terms both models take seconds per instance on formulas with at
most a few thousand clauses, which MiniSat solves in a small fraction of a
second. At this scale neither model is competitive with the solver in running
time, and the reduction of the iterations measures the quality of the decisions
(their convenience being a different matter). This is the motivation of the restricted heuristics
of \cite{Shirokikh2023} (cf. section~\ref{sec:impl}) and of the offline
inference of \cite{Wang2024}, whose benefit may only show on instances where the
search is long enough to dominate the cost of inference.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conc}
%% ===========================================================================

We have added two edge-featured graph attention layers to the core block of
Graph-Q-SAT, and compared the resulting model, GAT-Q-SAT, with Graph-Q-SAT at
matched training set and configuration. On all the graph-coloring families but
the smallest one, where the two were level, GAT-Q-SAT took fewer decisions. The
difference was small on instances of about the training size and large (up to
about $1.5$) on those three and four times larger, where Graph-Q-SAT approached
the reference solver. A Graph-Q-SAT with as many parameters as GAT-Q-SAT did
not close this gap, attention that replaced the sum of the messages, instead of
being added to it, was worse than Graph-Q-SAT on all the families, and
attention in the pooling of the global attribute did not improve GAT-Q-SAT. GAT-Q-SAT was also the better of the two when
trained on random formulas and tested on graph coloring and, with the caveats
due to the size of the families, on some of the structured families that
neither model had seen. On satisfiable uniform random 3-SAT, instead,
Graph-Q-SAT was clearly superior, consistently with the absence of structure
for the attention to weight. On the unsatisfiable formulas of the same
generator, however, the two were level or GAT-Q-SAT was ahead. Within the limits of these experiments, the attention layers
seem to make the learned heuristic less sensitive to the size and to the nature
of the test instances when these have some structure, at the cost of about 60\%
more parameters and of a more expensive decision.

\smallskip
\noindent
Several limitations should be kept in mind. First, five training seeds per
variant (two or three for the controls) are enough for the larger differences
but not for those of a few tenths of MRIR, which may well be within the
variance of DQN training. Second, the controls tell the attention apart from the
capacity only for the two forms that we tried: the loss of the degree is a
plausible explanation of the failure of attention aggregation, but we have not
tested it, e.g., by passing the degree to the weighted mean as a feature. On the instances used here neither model is competitive with MiniSat
in running time, and the restricted heuristics that should reduce the cost of
inference are implemented but have not been measured on instances large enough
for them to matter. Finally, several families of the transfer panel have fewer
than 10 instances, and the largest instances of SATLIB (e.g., those of bounded
model checking) are beyond the size that we could evaluate, which is perhaps
the most serious limit of the panel; a panel of larger
industrial instances, e.g., that of \cite{Li2024}, would be the natural next
test, and we hope to address it in the future.

\section{The instances, and how the numbers are regenerated}\label{app:instances}
%% ===========================================================================

All the instances come from SATLIB \cite{HoosStutzle2000}, in DIMACS form, and
are stored in the repository of the code (cf. section~\ref{sec:intro}). The families used in training
(\code{flat50-115}, \code{uf50-218}, \code{uf100-430}, \code{uuf50-218} and
\code{uuf100-430}) are split once, with a fixed seed, into training, validation
and test parts of 80\%, 10\% and 10\% (800, 100 and 100 formulas for most of
them). The evaluations of section~\ref{sec:results} use the test parts of these
families and the whole of the others, i.e., \code{flat30-60},
\code{flat75-180}, \code{flat100-239}, \code{flat125-301}, \code{flat150-360},
\code{flat175-417} and \code{flat200-479} for graph coloring, and
\code{uf250-1065} and \code{uuf250-1065} for random 3-SAT, with 100 formulas
each (101 for \code{flat150-360}).

\smallskip
\noindent
The panel of section~\ref{sec:transfer} contains all the structured SATLIB
families with at most 2000 variables and 150000 clauses per instance, namely
AIM (72 instances with 50, 100 and 200 variables, 48 satisfiable with a single
solution and 24 unsatisfiable), \code{jnh} (50, random with clauses of variable
length), \code{dubois} (13, unsatisfiable), \code{pret} (8, unsatisfiable
2-coloring), pigeon hole (4, unsatisfiable), parity learning (20: \code{par8}
and \code{par16}), all interval series (4),
inductive inference (40), circuit fault analysis (9: the \code{bf} and
\code{ssa} instances within the limit), the Beijing set (8), towers of Hanoi
(1), planning (8, blocks world and logistics) and quasigroup completion (20),
i.e., 257 instances. The limit of 10 minutes on MiniSat leaves out 7 further
instances within the bounds on the size (the five compressed \code{par32},
\code{hole10} and \code{hanoi5}), which MiniSat does not solve in that time;
we also leave out the inductive inference instance \code{ii16d1}, which MiniSat
solves in 665 decisions but which, after the 200 decisions of the fourth seed
of Graph-Q-SAT, MiniSat with no restarts does not solve within an hour, so that
no model could be compared on it with all its seeds.
The rule leaves out all of \code{bmc} and of the large graph coloring set
\code{gcp}, the uncompressed \code{par32}, the largest blocks-world, logistics,
quasigroup and circuit instances and most of the Beijing set; the large random
formulas of \code{LRAN} are not structured, and are not used. The small-world
set contains 20 instances for each of the nine SATLIB levels
\code{sw100-8-lp0-c5} to \code{sw100-8-lp8-c5} (drawn with a fixed seed from the
100 of each level; level $k$ has rewiring probability $2^{-k}$) and the single
lattice instance \code{sw100-8-p0-c5}, all with 500 variables and 3100 clauses.
None of these instances is used in training.

\smallskip
\noindent
For each family the iterations of MiniSat with and without restarts are
computed once, instance by instance, and stored with the family, so that the
denominator of \eqref{eq:mrir} is the same for all the models; for the families
of section~\ref{sec:transfer} this is done by \code{make\_metadata.py}, which
runs the two MiniSat configurations on each instance with a limit of 10 minutes
and leaves out (listing it) any instance that is not solved within the limit
(the 7 instances above). The evaluation
of a model on a family at a cap writes one line per instance, with the time, the
iterations of the model and the two iterations of MiniSat. The logs of the
runs trained on random formulas record only the ratio against MiniSat without
restarts. The tables of section~\ref{sec:results} are printed
by \code{paper\_numbers.py}, which reads the logs, groups the runs by variant
and training family, and applies the choice of the denominator described at the
beginning of section~\ref{sec:results}. The figures are drawn from the same
logs, and with the same grouping, by \code{make\_plots.py}
(Figures~\ref{fig:flatpair} and~\ref{fig:randpair}), \code{paper\_analysis.py}
(Figures~\ref{fig:thesis} and~\ref{fig:gen}) and \code{transfer\_study.py}
(Figure~\ref{fig:transfer}), which also runs the evaluations of
section~\ref{sec:transfer}; those of the other sections are run by
\code{reeval.sh}. All the logs are in the repository, next to each run
(\code{runs/<run>/reeval} and \code{runs/<run>/transfer}), and therefore the
tables and the figures can be regenerated without evaluating any model again.
