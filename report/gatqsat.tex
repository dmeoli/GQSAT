\begin{abstract}
We study the branching heuristic of a Conflict-Driven Clause Learning solver
for the Boolean satisfiability problem when it is learned by reinforcement, as
in Graph-Q-SAT, where a graph network reads the bipartite graph of the formula
and is trained by Deep Q-Learning to reduce the number of decisions of the
solver. We add to the core block of that network two edge-featured graph
attention layers, which weight the neighbours of each node by learned
coefficients before the global attribute is updated, and we compare the
resulting model with the plain one at matched training set and configuration.
On graph colouring the attention model takes fewer decisions than the plain one
on all the families we test, and the difference tends to grow with the size of
the instances, since the plain model degrades almost to the level of the
reference solver on the largest ones. Trained on uniform random 3-SAT and tested
on graph colouring, the attention model is again the better of the two, and it
also appears to be the better one on some structured families that neither
model has seen in training. Yet, on satisfiable uniform random 3-SAT the plain
model is clearly superior, and on instances of this size neither model is
competitive with the solver in running time, each decision costing a forward
pass of the network. This evidence rests on two training seeds per variant on
graph colouring, and on one or two elsewhere. Computational results are
reported.
\end{abstract}

%% ===========================================================================
\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%% ===========================================================================

We are concerned with the branching heuristic of a solver for the Boolean
satisfiability problem (SAT), i.e., the problem of deciding whether a formula in
conjunctive normal form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cnf}
  \varphi \;=\; \bigwedge_{j=1}^{m} \; \bigvee_{\ell \in C_j} \ell
\end{equation}
over $n$ Boolean variables admits an assignment that satisfies all the clauses
$C_j$ (each literal $\ell$ being a variable or its negation). We consider
solvers that implement Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL): the search
repeatedly (i) decides the value of an unassigned variable, (ii) propagates the
assignments implied by the clauses, and (iii) upon a conflict learns a new
clause and backjumps to the level that the clause allows
\cite{MarquesSilva1999,MarquesSilva2021}. In practice the choice at point (i)
is delegated to a heuristic, usually some variant of the Variable State Independent Decaying
Sum (VSIDS) \cite{Moskewicz2001} (see, e.g., \cite{Liang2016} for an
alternative), which ranks the variables by activity counters that are increased
at each conflict and periodically decayed. These counters usually carry little
information at the start of the search. Furthermore, they are only a rough surrogate of
what one would like to know (say, the variable whose assignment leads to the
smallest search tree). The heuristic is therefore a natural place for a learned
policy, and a convenient one, since a poor decision costs time but not
correctness (the solver is sound and complete whatever the policy does). We
restrict ourselves to the choice of the variable and of its value, and we leave
restarts, clause deletion and preprocessing as they are in MiniSat
\cite{EenSorensson2003}.

\smallskip
\noindent
Learning for SAT has a large literature, which cannot be reviewed here; we
refer to \cite{Guo2023} for a survey and to \cite{Li2023} for a benchmark of
graph networks on SAT problems, and only recall the works that bear on ours.
NeuroSAT \cite{Selsam2019} predicts satisfiability from single-bit
supervision, which shows that message passing \cite{Gilmer2017} over the graph
of a formula captures some of its structure. NeuroCore \cite{SelsamBjorner2019}
predicts the variables that occur in unsatisfiable cores and uses the
prediction to re-rank the VSIDS activities periodically, while NeuroBack
\cite{Wang2024} queries the network once, before the search, so that inference
is paid offline. Other works learn heuristics for local search
\cite{YolcuPoczos2019,Zhang2020}. Learning the branching rule by reinforcement
goes back at least to \cite{LagoudakisLittman2001}, and the
analogous problem for Mixed-Integer Programs has been approached with
imitation learning and graph networks \cite{Khalil2016,Gasse2019} (cf.
\cite{Bengio2021} for a survey of learning in combinatorial optimization).
Graph-Q-SAT \cite{Kurin2020}, on which we build, represents the state of the
search as the bipartite graph of the formula, and learns the action-value
function of the corresponding decision process with Deep Q-Learning (DQN)
\cite{Mnih2015}. Since the network reads a graph, one set of weights applies to
formulas of any size, and the heuristic can be evaluated on instances larger
than (or different from) the training ones. This model has been extended with
new graph representations and with ``restricted'' heuristics that query the
network on part of the decisions only \cite{Shirokikh2023}, and
\cite{Mojzisek2025} studies how much of the reported gains depends on the
experimental choices around the method. Graph attention
\cite{Velickovic2018,Brody2022} appears in some of the works above but, to the
best of our knowledge, not inside the core block of Graph-Q-SAT.

\smallskip
\noindent
In this note we add to the core block of Graph-Q-SAT two graph attention layers
that use the edge embeddings computed by the block, and we compare the
resulting model (GAT-Q-SAT) with the plain one. We make the comparison at
matched training set and configuration, on graph colouring (the family on which
Graph-Q-SAT was originally trained, and larger instances of it), on uniform
random 3-SAT and, with no retraining, on four structured families that neither
model has seen. We remark that the attention layers are added to the block, the
sum aggregation of Graph-Q-SAT being kept; hence, what we measure is the effect
of the added layers (and of the parameters that come with them). We use two
training seeds per variant on graph colouring and one or two on the other
families, which is enough to see the larger differences but not to put an
error bar on the smaller ones (i.e., those of a few tenths of the measure used
below). As in \cite{Kurin2020} the results are measured
in solver iterations, and the running time is reported separately. The note
also documents a port of the original implementation to a current software
stack (without which the experiments could not have been run), together with
the test used to check that the port has not changed what the models compute.

\smallskip
\noindent
The structure of the note is as follows. In section~\ref{sec:problem} we recall
the decision process defined by the branching problem and the algorithm used to
learn it, with the training configuration of all the runs. Section~\ref{sec:gqsat}
describes the graph representation of the state and the network of Graph-Q-SAT,
and section~\ref{sec:gat} the attention layers that GAT-Q-SAT adds to it; the
port and the reproduction test are described in section~\ref{sec:impl}. In
section~\ref{sec:results} we report the computational results, ordered by the
distance between the training and the test instances (graph colouring at
increasing size, a change of the training distribution, a change of domain),
followed by uniform random 3-SAT and by the running time. Finally, in
section~\ref{sec:conc} we draw some conclusions, and the appendix describes the
instances and how the numbers of section~\ref{sec:results} are regenerated.
Instance families keep their SATLIB \cite{HoosStutzle2000} names:
\code{uf}$n$-$m$ and \code{uuf}$n$-$m$ are satisfiable and unsatisfiable
uniform random 3-SAT with $n$ variables and $m$ clauses, and \code{flat}$n$-$m$
are the 3-colouring problems of graphs with $n$ nodes and $m$ edges, generated
as in \cite{CulbersonLuo1996}.

\section{Branching as a decision process}\label{sec:problem}
%% ===========================================================================

Following \cite{Kurin2020}, the solver is the environment of a Markov Decision
Process. Its state is the formula \eqref{eq:cnf} simplified by the current
partial assignment (together with the clauses learned so far), and its actions
are the pairs of an unassigned variable and a truth value. Transitions are
computed by the solver itself (cf. \cite[section 3]{Kurin2020}): a step is one decision followed by the propagation, the
conflict analysis and the backjumping that it triggers, and therefore the
transition is deterministic but known only by running it. A reward of $-p$ is
given at each non-terminal step and $0$ at termination (with $p = 0.1$ in all our
runs); hence, maximizing the return means terminating in as few decisions as
possible. Note that the formula is first simplified by the variable elimination
of MiniSat, which is part of the environment.

\smallskip
\noindent
The action-value function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bellman}
  Q^\star(s,a) = \mathbb{E}\big[\,r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^\star(s',a')\,\big]
\end{equation}
is approximated by a network $Q_\theta$ trained by DQN \cite{Mnih2015}. This
minimizes the squared one-step temporal-difference error on minibatches drawn
uniformly from a replay buffer, with the bootstrap target computed by a copy
$\theta^-$ of the weights that is refreshed periodically, and with the
transitions collected by an $\varepsilon$-greedy policy (neither the double
estimator of \cite{vanHasselt2016} nor the prioritized replay of
\cite{Schaul2016} is used); the learned heuristic is then
$\pi(s) = \arg\max_a Q_\theta(s,a)$. Two features of the problem constrain the
approximator: the number of actions changes along an episode (two per
unassigned variable), and the state is defined up to a permutation of the
variables and of the clauses. Hence, the network must score a variable
number of actions and be invariant under those permutations, which rules out
fixed-size encodings such as that of \cite{WangRompf2018} and leads to the
graph representation of section~\ref{sec:gqsat}.

\smallskip
\noindent
All the runs of this note use the configuration of \cite{Kurin2020}, which we
list here since it is not repeated elsewhere. Training uses Adam with a
constant learning rate of $2\cdot 10^{-5}$, the discount is $\gamma = 0.99$,
the replay buffer holds 20000 transitions and the minibatches have 64 of them.
One update is made every 4 transitions after the first 5000, and $\theta$ is copied
into $\theta^-$ every 10 updates. Training stops after 50000 updates (about
$2\cdot 10^{5}$ transitions); $\varepsilon$ is $1$ for the first 5000
transitions and then decreases linearly to $0.01$. During training an episode
may take at most 500 decisions of the model, after which the solver completes
the search with VSIDS in a single step. Also, the gradient norm is clipped at $0.1$
for the runs trained on graph colouring and at $1$ for those trained on uniform
random 3-SAT (as in the respective configurations of \cite{Kurin2020}).

%% ===========================================================================
\section{Graph-Q-SAT}\label{sec:gqsat}
%% ===========================================================================

The state is represented \cite{Kurin2020} as a bipartite graph whose nodes are
the unassigned variables and the clauses (original and learned) that are not
yet satisfied, each clause restricted to its literals that are not false. There
is an edge between a variable and each clause in which it occurs (stored in
both directions). Node features are 2-dimensional one-hot vectors that
distinguish the two kinds of node, edge features encode in the same way the
polarity of the literal, and the global attribute is a constant. On each
variable node the network produces two $Q$-values on each variable node (one per truth value), and the
agent takes the $\arg\max$ over all of them.

\smallskip
\noindent
As in \cite{Kurin2020}, the network is an encode--process--decode graph network
\cite{Battaglia2018} (cf. Figure~\ref{fig:epd}). An encoder maps the edges, the nodes and the global
attribute independently to embeddings of size 32. Then, the core block (whose
weights are shared) is applied $M = 4$ times to the concatenation of the encoded
graph and of its previous output. Each application updates first the edges,
then the nodes, then the global attribute:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gnblock}
  e_k' = \phi^e\big(e_k, v_{r_k}, v_{s_k}, u\big) \, , \quad
  v_i' = \phi^v\Big(\textstyle\sum_{k:\, r_k = i} e_k', \, v_i, \, u\Big) \, , \quad
  u'   = \phi^u\big(\bar e', \bar v', u\big) \, ,
\end{equation}
where $r_k$ and $s_k$ are the two endpoints of edge $k$, $\bar e'$ and $\bar v'$
are the means of the updated edge and node embeddings, and $\phi^e$, $\phi^v$
and $\phi^u$ are perceptrons with one hidden layer of 64 units and outputs of
size 64, each followed by a layer normalization. Finally, a decoder reads the
$Q$-values off the variable nodes. Since the weights are shared across the nodes
and across the $M$ rounds, one set of them applies to a formula with any number
of variables and clauses; with this configuration the network has 71144
parameters.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{gqsat_epd.png}
\caption{The encode--process--decode network of Graph-Q-SAT: an independent
block encodes the edges, the nodes and the global attribute, a full graph-network
block is applied $M$ times with shared weights, and a second independent block
decodes the $Q$-values. Blocks drawn after \cite{Battaglia2018}.}
\label{fig:epd}
\end{figure}

\smallskip
\noindent
Note that the node update of \eqref{eq:gnblock} sums the messages of the
edges incident to a node, and therefore it gives the same weight to all the clauses in which a
variable occurs (and to all the variables of a clause). For a formula drawn
uniformly at random at the phase transition \cite{Mitchell1992} this seems a
reasonable choice, the clauses being statistically alike. In a formula that
encodes a structured problem, instead, the clauses of a variable often play
different roles (in graph colouring, e.g., ``at least one colour'' for a node
and ``different colours'' for the two ends of an edge), and one may expect a
weighting of the neighbours to be of some use.

%% ===========================================================================
\section{GAT-Q-SAT}\label{sec:gat}
%% ===========================================================================

GAT-Q-SAT inserts two graph attention layers \cite{Velickovic2018} in the core
block, between the node update and the global update of \eqref{eq:gnblock}.
Thus, the node embeddings are recomputed from those of their neighbours, with
learned weights, before $u'$ is computed. Each layer maps the embeddings $h_i$
of the nodes into
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gat}
  h_i^{+} = \mathop{\Big\|}_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{j \in \mathcal N_i} \alpha^k_{ij}\, W_k h_j + b \, ,
  \qquad
  \alpha^k_{ij} = \frac{\exp s^k_{ij}}{\sum_{l \in \mathcal N_i} \exp s^k_{il}} \, ,
  \qquad
  s^k_{ij} = \mathrm{LeakyReLU}\big(\mathbf a_k^{\!\top}
            [\,W_k h_i \,\|\, W_k h_j \,\|\, W^e_k e'_{ij}\,]\big) \, ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal N_i$ is the set of the neighbours of $i$ in the bipartite graph
(with no self-loop), $\|$ denotes concatenation, and $e'_{ij}$ is the embedding
of the edge between $i$ and $j$ computed by $\phi^e$ in the same application of
the block. Hence, the coefficients $\alpha^k_{ij}$ depend on the current edge
embeddings, which carry the polarity of the literal together with what the
block has added to it. In our configuration the first layer has $K = 3$ heads
of size 64 (concatenated into 192 components) and the second a single head,
which returns to size 64; each layer is followed by a ReLU, and the output by a
layer normalization. All the rest of the network (the encoder, the edge and node updates
with their sum, the global update, the decoder, and $M$) is unchanged, and so is
the training configuration of section~\ref{sec:problem}. In the terms of
\cite{Battaglia2018}, the resulting block is a full graph-network block with a
non-local block inserted between the node and the global update
(cf. Figure~\ref{fig:gatepd}).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{gatqsat_epd.png}
\caption{The core block of GAT-Q-SAT: the block of Figure~\ref{fig:epd} with a
non-local block of two attention layers \eqref{eq:gat} inserted between the
node and the global update. Blocks drawn after \cite{Battaglia2018}.}
\label{fig:gatepd}
\end{figure}

\smallskip
\noindent
The attention layers add 42112 parameters to the 71144 of Graph-Q-SAT (about
60\% more), and their cost is paid at each decision, since the coefficients are
computed on all the edges at each application of the block. Of course, the
coefficients can only be of use where the neighbours of a node differ in a way
that matters for the decision. On a uniform random formula at the phase
transition one may expect them to learn little (if anything), and the added
parameters to make the fit somewhat harder. Note also that, since the layers are added and the
sum of \eqref{eq:gnblock} is kept, section~\ref{sec:results} compares the plain
model with the same model plus a (larger) attention stage. Comparing two
aggregation rules at the same capacity would require replacing the sum by the
attention inside $\phi^v$, which we have not done.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}
%% ===========================================================================

The original implementation of \cite{Kurin2020} is written in PyTorch~1.1 with pinned
versions of PyTorch Geometric \cite{FeyLenssen2019} and of the compiled
extensions \code{torch-scatter} and \code{torch-sparse}. It drives a modified
MiniSat~2.2 \cite{EenSorensson2003}, exposed to Python as a \code{gym}
environment through a SWIG wrapper. These versions can no longer be installed
together on a current system, and therefore we ported the code to current
releases of PyTorch and PyTorch Geometric. In the port the aggregations of
\eqref{eq:gnblock} are taken from \code{torch\_geometric.utils.scatter}, which
removes the two compiled extensions. The environment is constructed
directly, without \code{gym.make} (whose checks reject the non-standard
signatures of the MiniSat wrapper), which allows the same code to run with
\code{gym} and with \code{gymnasium}; the SWIG wrapper is regenerated and
rebuilt against \code{numpy}~2. Also, the version of PyTorch Geometric used in 2021
stores the linear map of an attention layer twice (\code{lin\_src} and
\code{lin\_dst}), where the current one has a single \code{lin}. When a
checkpoint of 2021 is loaded one of the two copies is kept; this loses nothing,
since in all our checkpoints the two are equal.

\smallskip
\noindent
In order to check that the port has not changed what the models compute, we
re-ran on the new stack the evaluation of the checkpoints trained in 2021 on the
original one, and compared the logs instance by instance. In 63 of the 86
repeated evaluations the number of iterations of the model coincides with the
2021 one on all the instances; in the other 23 the median ratio
\eqref{eq:mrir} changes by at most $0.03$ (on \code{flat30-60} at cap $500$,
e.g., the medians are $1.833$ for Graph-Q-SAT and $2.000$ for GAT-Q-SAT on both
stacks). We have not traced the residual differences, which may be due to the
non-determinism of the scatter operations on a GPU; they are in any case much
smaller than the differences discussed in section~\ref{sec:results}. Indeed, inference
exercises the graph construction, the message passing, the decoder and the
stepping of the environment together, and hence this is a reasonably stringent
test of all but the training path. The latter was checked by retraining the two
colouring models from scratch on the new stack (these are the second seeds of
section~\ref{sec:results}).

\smallskip
\noindent
Finally, the evaluation code implements the three restricted heuristics of
\cite{Shirokikh2023}, which reduce the number of forward passes. In the first
one the model takes the first $N$ decisions and VSIDS the rest. In the second
one a forward pass gives the $K$ best actions, which are executed in order
(skipping those whose variable has meanwhile been assigned) before the network
is queried again. In the third one the $Q$-values computed at the root
initialize the activities of VSIDS, which then runs unaided (this required a
new method of the solver, which writes the activities and rebuilds the order
heap). We do not report results for them, since on the instances of
section~\ref{sec:results} the search is too short for the saving in inference
to show.

%% ===========================================================================
\section{Computational results}\label{sec:results}
%% ===========================================================================

We now compare the two models on test instances that are increasingly far from
the training ones. As in \cite{Kurin2020}, the quality of a heuristic is
measured by the Median Relative Iteration Reduction
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mrir}
  \MRIR = \operatorname{median}\,
          \frac{\#\text{iterations of MiniSat}}{\#\text{iterations of the model}} \, ,
\end{equation}
where the median is over the instances of a family, and an iteration is a
branching decision (of the model or of VSIDS). Each evaluation is run with a
``cap'' on the number of decisions of the model, after which VSIDS takes the
remaining ones; unless stated otherwise the cap is $500$, as in training.

\smallskip
\noindent
{\bf Reference solver.} The model-guided solver runs without
restarts, while MiniSat can be run with or without them (with the restart
sequence of \cite{Luby1993}), and the choice of the denominator of
\eqref{eq:mrir} is by no means inconsequential. On graph colouring MiniSat with
restarts takes up to 26\% fewer iterations than without (on
\code{flat175-417}); on \code{uf250-1065} and \code{uuf250-1065}, however, it
takes 2.2 and 2.9 times more. We divide by the stronger of the two on each
family, i.e., by MiniSat with restarts on graph colouring (as in
\cite{Kurin2020}) and without restarts on uniform random 3-SAT. The one
exception is \code{uuf100-430}, where the run with restarts takes 6\% fewer
iterations but where our logs (those of 2021, the only ones for the random
families) record only the other one; hence, the MRIR reported there is slightly
favourable to the models. On the families of
section~\ref{sec:transfer} we also divide by the stronger of the two.

\smallskip
\noindent
{\bf Runs.} Each run is trained once on one family, with the configuration of
section~\ref{sec:problem}, and is evaluated at its last checkpoint. On graph
colouring each variant has two runs trained on \code{flat50-115}, i.e., two
seeds, one trained in 2021 on the original stack and one in 2026 on the ported
one. On uniform random 3-SAT each variant has two runs trained on satisfiable
formulas (one on \code{uf50-218}, one on \code{uf100-430}), whose results we
average, and two trained on the unsatisfiable \code{uuf50-218} and
\code{uuf100-430}, used only as a control in section~\ref{sec:random}. For a
group of runs we report the mean of the MRIR of its runs.

\smallskip
\noindent
{\bf Instances and machine.} Each test set has 100 instances (101 for
\code{flat150-360}): the held-out test part for the families used in training,
and the whole family for the others (cf. the appendix). All the evaluations
of 2026 were run on a Colab instance with a GPU, whose load we could not
control. This does not affect the MRIR (a ratio of iteration counts), whereas the running
times of section~\ref{sec:time} are, and we discuss their reliability there.

longer, and the ranking of Table~\ref{tab:numbers} does not seem to depend on
the choice of the cap.

\subsection{A change of the training distribution}\label{sec:shift}

We now evaluate on graph colouring the models trained on satisfiable uniform
random 3-SAT, whose formulas have none of the structure of a colouring
problem. On five of the eight families the plain model is below the reference
solver, its MRIR ranging from $0.63$ to $1.16$ ($0.89$ on average). The
attention model, instead, is above it on seven (from $0.94$ to $1.75$, $1.44$ on average);
the difference between the two is $0.44$ to $0.97$ on the first seven
families. On \code{flat200-479}, however, it drops to $0.10$, and the attention
model too falls slightly below parity. Figure~\ref{fig:thesis} compares at cap
$500$ the three regimes that have been measured on the same families, and
Figure~\ref{fig:gen} shows the models trained on random formulas as a function
of the number of variables of the test instances.

here than in section~\ref{sec:indist} (\upd{$0.55$ against $0.78$} on average),
and that the MRIR of both models decreases with the size of the colouring
instances.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{A change of domain}\label{sec:transfer}

All the families above are random, either in the formula or in the graph.
They say little about what a user of a learned heuristic would like to know,
namely whether a heuristic trained on the instances at hand is of any use on
different ones. We therefore evaluated the colouring-trained models of both
seeds (with no retraining) on four SATLIB families of a different nature.
These are
small-world graph colouring \cite{Walsh1999} (a change of the topology of the
graph within the same problem), the AIM instances \cite{Asahiro1996}
(structured 3-SAT with at most one solution), quasigroup completion
\cite{GomesSelman1997}, and planning \cite{KautzSelman1996} (blocks world and
logistics). These families are ``small'' (60, 72, 11 and 8 instances
respectively), and we evaluate them at a cap of $200$ to contain the cost of the
largest planning and quasigroup instances. Figure~\ref{fig:transfer} reports
the MRIR, divided by the stronger MiniSat run on each family (with restarts on
small-world colouring and on quasigroups, without on planning, the two being
equal on AIM).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{transfer.png}
\caption{Colouring-trained models on four families not seen in training: MRIR
at cap $200$ against the stronger MiniSat run on each family, mean over the two
seeds.}
\label{fig:transfer}
\end{figure}

\noindent
\upd{On AIM ($1.49$),
quasigroups ($1.08$) and planning ($1.14$) the attention model is above the
reference solver, and the plain one only on AIM ($1.50$). On small-world colouring, which is the closest of the four to the
training problem, both are below it ($0.58$ and $0.81$).} On AIM the two models
are basically equivalent, while the advantage of the attention model on
quasigroups and planning rests on 11 and 8 instances, and it should therefore be
taken as an indication only. Our result on small-world colouring is somewhat
unexpected, since a change of topology within the same problem hurts both models
more than a change of problem does. We do not have an explanation for it. One
may speculate that the models might be sensitive to the degree distribution of the
graph, which is very different in the (almost regular) small-world graphs and in
the random graphs of the \code{flat} families.

\subsection{Uniform random 3-SAT}\label{sec:random}

On uniform random 3-SAT the ranking of the two models is reversed.
Table~\ref{tab:unsat} reports the MRIR of the models trained on satisfiable
formulas (mean of the runs trained on \code{uf50-218} and \code{uf100-430}) on

there, on instances five times larger than the training ones.) This is
consistent with the remark of section~\ref{sec:gat}: at the phase transition the
neighbours of a node are statistically alike, and the attention coefficients

\subsection{Iterations and running time}\label{sec:time}

The MRIR counts iterations, which are the quantity on which the heuristic acts.
However, a decision of the model costs a forward pass of the network on the
current graph, and the attention layers make that pass more expensive. We
measure the time per instance around the episode. It includes the
parsing of the formula, the variable elimination, the construction of the graph
at each decision, the forward passes and the part of the search left to VSIDS
(but not the loading of the model and the runs of MiniSat that give
the denominator of \eqref{eq:mrir}, which are computed once per family and
stored with it). We report medians over the instances, since a few instances
usually take much longer than the others. As said above, the machine was not under our
control: \upd{repeating two of the evaluations gave medians within $X$\% of the
first ones}, and hence we only comment on differences much larger than that.

Table~\ref{tab:budget} reports time and MRIR on the largest colouring family at
three values of the cap. At cap $50$ a decision of GAT-Q-SAT is more expensive
than one of Graph-Q-SAT (\upd{$3.52$ against $2.55$} seconds for the same number
of model decisions), as expected. At larger caps, instead, the attention model
is the faster of the two (\upd{by a factor of 2 at cap $500$ and of almost 5 at
cap $1000$}), since it terminates in fewer decisions; indeed, each decision of a
model costs far more than the VSIDS decisions it replaces. The plain model pays
\upd{22 times} the time of cap $50$ to reach a lower MRIR, and it ends up below the reference
solver at cap $1000$. Going from cap $50$ to cap $500$ improves the attention
model by \upd{$0.21$}, and going to $1000$ changes nothing, since its episodes
terminate before the cap is reached.

\smallskip
\noindent
In absolute terms both models take seconds per instance on formulas with at
most a few thousand clauses, which MiniSat solves in a small fraction of a
second. At this scale neither model is competitive with the solver in running
time, and the reduction of the iterations measures the quality of the decisions
(their convenience being a different matter). This is the motivation of the restricted heuristics
of \cite{Shirokikh2023} (cf. section~\ref{sec:impl}) and of the offline
inference of \cite{Wang2024}, whose benefit may only show on instances where the
search is long enough to dominate the cost of inference.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conc}
%% ===========================================================================

We have added two edge-featured graph attention layers to the core block of
Graph-Q-SAT, and compared the resulting model with the plain one at matched
training set and configuration. On all the graph-colouring families the attention
model took fewer decisions. The difference was small on instances of about
the training size and large (up to about $1.7$) on those three and four times larger, where the
plain model approached the reference solver. It was also the better of the two when trained on random formulas and tested on graph colouring
and, with the caveats due to the size of the families, on some of the
structured families that neither model had seen. On satisfiable uniform random
3-SAT, instead, the plain model was clearly superior, consistently with the
absence of structure for the attention to weight. On the unsatisfiable
formulas of the same generator, however, the two were level or the attention
model was slightly ahead. Within the limits of these experiments, the attention layers
seem to make the learned heuristic less sensitive to the size and to the nature
of the test instances when these have some structure, at the cost of about 60\%
more parameters and of a more expensive decision.

\smallskip
\noindent
Several limitations should be kept in mind. First, the comparison involves two
training seeds per variant on graph colouring and one or two on the other
families. The transfer study rests on four ``small'' families, two of which
have 11 and 8 instances; hence, differences of a few tenths of MRIR may well be within
the variance of DQN training. Furthermore, since the attention layers are added to the
block, the comparison does not separate the effect of the weighting of the
neighbours from that of the added capacity, which may well account for part of
the difference; replacing the sum of
\eqref{eq:gnblock} by the attention, at a comparable number of parameters, would
settle this point. On the instances used here neither model is competitive with
MiniSat in running time. Also, the restricted heuristics that should reduce the
cost of inference are implemented but have not been measured on instances large
enough for them to matter. Finally, the poor transfer of both models to
small-world colouring (the family closest to the training one) is not explained.
It is perhaps the first thing that a larger panel of instances, e.g., that
of \cite{Li2023}, should clarify; we hope to address some of these points in the
future.

\section{The instances, and how the numbers are regenerated}\label{app:instances}
%% ===========================================================================

All the instances come from SATLIB \cite{HoosStutzle2000}, in DIMACS form, and
are stored in the repository of the note. The families used in training
(\code{flat50-115}, \code{uf50-218}, \code{uf100-430}, \code{uuf50-218} and
\code{uuf100-430}) are split once, with a fixed seed, into training, validation
and test parts of 80\%, 10\% and 10\% (800, 100 and 100 formulas for most of
them). The evaluations of section~\ref{sec:results} use the test parts of these
families and the whole of the others, i.e., \code{flat30-60},
\code{flat75-180}, \code{flat100-239}, \code{flat125-301}, \code{flat150-360},
\code{flat175-417} and \code{flat200-479} for graph colouring, and
\code{uf250-1065} and \code{uuf250-1065} for random 3-SAT, with 100 formulas
each (101 for \code{flat150-360}).

\smallskip
\noindent
The four families of section~\ref{sec:transfer} are the following: (i)
small-world colouring, 60 instances with 500 variables and 3100 clauses,
obtained by 5-colouring graphs on 100 vertices whose rewiring parameter moves
them from a ring lattice towards a random graph (the six least random levels of
SATLIB, 10 instances each); (ii) AIM, 72 instances with 50, 100 and 200
variables, 48 satisfiable with a single solution and 24 unsatisfiable; (iii)
quasigroup completion, 11 instances with 343 to 729 variables and up to about
$1.5\cdot 10^5$ clauses; (iv) planning, 8 instances with 48 to 1141 variables
from the blocks-world and logistics encodings. None of them is used in
training.

\smallskip
\noindent
For each family the iterations of MiniSat with and without restarts are
computed once, instance by instance, and stored with the family, so that the
denominator of \eqref{eq:mrir} is the same for all the models. The evaluation
of a model on a family at a cap writes one line per instance, with the time, the
iterations of the model and the two iterations of MiniSat. The logs of 2021,
which are the only ones for the random families, record only the ratio against
MiniSat without restarts. The tables of section~\ref{sec:results} are printed
by \code{paper\_numbers.py}, which reads the logs, groups the runs by variant
and training family, and applies the choice of the denominator described at the
beginning of section~\ref{sec:results}. The figures are drawn from the same
logs, and with the same grouping, by \code{make\_plots.py}
(Figures~\ref{fig:flatpair} and~\ref{fig:randpair}), \code{paper\_analysis.py}
(Figures~\ref{fig:thesis} and~\ref{fig:gen}) and \code{transfer\_study.py}
(Figure~\ref{fig:transfer}), which also runs the evaluations of
section~\ref{sec:transfer}; those of the other sections are run by
\code{reeval.sh}. The logs written in 2026 are kept with the checkpoints,
outside the repository.
